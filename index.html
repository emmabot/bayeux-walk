    How it survived 950 years

    • 1476The first written record. An inventory of Bayeux Cathedral lists a long embroidered hanging of the Conquest, put up around the nave each summer for the feast of the relics.
    • 1729Scholars in Paris publish drawings of part of it, and the rest of Europe starts to take notice.
    • 1792During the Revolution it is commandeered to cover a military wagon. A local lawyer, Lambert Léonard-Leforestier, swaps in other cloth and hides it.
    • 1803Napoleon has it shown in Paris to rally support for his own planned invasion of England. The invasion never happens, and the tapestry goes home.
    • 1886Thirty-five women of the Leek Embroidery Society finish a full-size copy for England. They discreetly clothe the few naked figures. It hangs today in Reading.
    • 1944Moved to the Louvre during the war. As Paris is liberated, the SS try to take it to Germany. It stays.
    • 2026It crosses the Channel the other way: on loan to the British Museum, back in England for the first time since it was made.

    About the tapestry

    An embroidery, strictly speaking: coloured wool stitched onto linen.

    The Bayeux Tapestry runs almost 70 metres and about half a metre high. It tells the story from Harold Godwinson's journey to Normandy through William's invasion and the Battle of Hastings in 1066, ending with the English in flight. It was probably commissioned by a Norman patron, stitched by English embroiderers, and drawn from manuscript models from Canterbury, sometime around 1072–77.

    At the British Museum

    On loan from France, it is back in England for the first time since it was made: Room 30, 10 September 2026 to 11 July 2027. Tickets through December are sold out; January to March 2027 tickets go on sale 21 October 2026. Exhibition page

    About this viewer

    This is the real tapestry, hung at true scale: the 2017 high-resolution photography by the Université de Caen Normandie, CNRS and ENSICAEN, published on Wikimedia Commons as public domain. The 56 scene photographs were joined into one continuous strip and cropped to the cloth, at about 10 pixels per centimetre. The captions group the traditional 58 scenes into 30 sections; the Latin is given with abbreviations expanded and spelling lightly regularised. The little ticket illustration is an original drawing in the tapestry's style.

    The extras

    The running count of people, horses and ships is an estimate spread across the scenes and scaled to the commonly cited totals of about 626 people, 190 horses and 41 ships. Published counts vary. The map is a rough sketch for orientation.
